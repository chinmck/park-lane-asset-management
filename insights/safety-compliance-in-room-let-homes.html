Safety compliance in room-let homes: the core legal checks

Parklane Insights · Asset Management · October 2026

Shared houses concentrate risk. More occupants, more appliances and shared escape routes mean safety failures have bigger consequences, and the law expects more of landlords and managers as a result. This article brings together the main recurring legal checks for privately rented shared homes in England, as set out in the legislation and official guidance.

1. Gas safety: every 12 months

Under the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998, landlords must: - have every gas appliance and flue they provide checked for safety at least every 12 months by a Gas Safe registered engineer; - keep the gas installation maintained in a safe condition; - give a copy of the gas safety record to existing tenants within 28 days of the check, and to new tenants before they move in; - keep records of each check.

The Health and Safety Executive (HSE) publishes the detailed duties for landlords. In an HMO, the HMO management regulations also require the manager to supply the latest gas safety certificate to the council within seven days if asked.

2. Electrical safety: at least every five years

The Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector (England) Regulations 2020 require landlords to: - have the fixed electrical installation inspected and tested by a qualified person at least every five years, and obtain a report, usually an EICR; - give the report to existing tenants within 28 days, to new tenants before they occupy, and to the council within seven days of a request; - complete any remedial or further investigative work (codes C1, C2 or FI) within 28 days, or sooner if the report says so, and then send written confirmation to the tenant and the council.

Government guidance published in November 2025 states that councils can impose financial penalties of up to £40,000 for breaches. The guidance confirms the regulations apply to shared houses, though not to communal areas of blocks, which are covered by other legislation.

3. Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms

Since 1 October 2022, the Smoke and Carbon Monoxide Alarm (Amendment) Regulations 2022 require landlords to: - fit at least one smoke alarm on every storey that has a room used as living accommodation; - fit a carbon monoxide alarm in any room used as living accommodation that contains a fixed combustion appliance (other than a gas cooker), such as a boiler or log burner; - check alarms work on the day a new tenancy begins, and repair or replace them once told they are faulty.

The guidance notes that licensed HMOs are covered by equivalent licence conditions, and that councils can fine up to £5,000 for each failure to comply with a remedial notice. Larger HMOs normally need more than the minimum. Fire safety in shared houses also falls under the Housing Act 2004 and fire safety legislation. A fire risk assessment of the common parts is standard practice.

4. HMO management duties

The Management of Houses in Multiple Occupation (England) Regulations 2006 apply to every HMO, licensed or not. Among other things, the manager must: - display their name, address and telephone number in a prominent position in the house; - keep fire escape routes free from obstruction and in good order, and maintain fire alarms and equipment; - keep the water supply, drainage, common parts, fixtures and fittings in good repair; - provide suitable waste storage and disposal facilities.

Failing to comply is a criminal offence, and councils can also impose civil penalties.

5. Energy performance

Under the Energy Efficiency (Private Rented Property) (England and Wales) Regulations 2015, a privately rented home generally needs an EPC rating of at least E unless a valid exemption is registered. An EPC must be provided to prospective tenants. Local schemes may also reward better ratings. In Liverpool and Wirral, for example, selective licence fees are reduced for homes rated C or above.

Making compliance routine

The common thread is records. Each duty requires the landlord to prove that a check was done, by whom and when, and that the results reached tenants and, if asked, the council. A simple compliance calendar per property makes this manageable: - gas safety date plus 12 months; - EICR date plus five years; - an alarm test record for each new tenancy; - licence expiry; - EPC expiry; - the date of the last fire risk review.

From 1 May 2026, the Renters' Rights Act also expanded rent repayment orders and councils' investigatory powers, so gaps in the record carry more risk than before.
